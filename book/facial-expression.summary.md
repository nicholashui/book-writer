## Part I — Foundations

### Preface

Subjective expectation forms the nervous system’s rapid, non-deliberate judgment of impending events from all available information, typically experienced as a feeling. It follows the primitive criterion of how things ought to be and leans toward negative expectations in dark or tense settings, aiding survival while increasing the value of positive outcomes. Positive expectations require more supporting information.

Emotion ABC theory, proposed by Ellis, holds that activating event A indirectly causes emotional and behavioral consequence C; belief B, formed by cognitive evaluation of A, is the direct cause. Negative emotions arise chiefly from erroneous beliefs. Emotions precede reason in micro-reaction research. The first neural response to stimuli is emotional; rational thought follows unless the stimulus overwhelms. Emotion operates on single-layer logic: positive response to beneficial stimuli and comparative judgment leading to rejection, confrontation, avoidance, or abandonment for harmful ones. Dictionary definitions separate enduring mood states (minutes to days) from situational, transient emotions proper (rarely exceeding a few seconds except sadness), the latter marked by external manifestations and used in micro-reaction analysis.

Driving scenarios illustrate expectation mismatches. A U.S. driver in America expects rule-based alternation and registers mild contempt at violations. The same driver in China encounters non-alternating traffic, producing surprise, contempt, anger, and eventual conformity. Experienced Chinese drivers hold flexible norms and experience fewer mismatches. A Chinese driver in the United States encounters repeated mismatches at uncontrolled intersections, generating confusion, fear, and observed disgust until local rules are explained. Expectation mismatch directly generates emotion: New Year countdown silence produces strangeness; unexpected candy yields delight; network disconnection yields cursing or anger when attributed to negligence.

Emotion management requires observing feelings inwardly, expressing impact without accusation, and releasing through confiding or music while avoiding repetition of error or self-harm. Inner emotions produce external expressions. Universal emotions are surprise, disgust, anger, fear, sadness, and happiness. Cognitive therapy, developed in the United States in the 1960s–1970s, alters maladaptive cognitions; three months produced significant improvement in 80 percent of unipolar-depression patients. Emotions follow 28-day cycles of peak, critical point, and trough. In dark environments, stimuli violating safety expectations produce intense fear; the same stimuli in bright environments yield weaker responses. Facial expressions do not necessarily indicate genuine emotion; actors can produce precise voluntary expressions. Effective stimulation tailored to the individual is required to elicit authentic emotion before micro-expressions are observed.

Terminology distinguishes an “actor” performing non-stress expressions from a “test subject” producing stress responses. The Facial Action Coding System (FACS) and its action units (AUs) provide the measurement framework. Micro-expressions last 1/25 to 1/4 second (approximately 0.2 seconds) and occur outside conscious control. Observed examples include asymmetric mouth lift during a commercialization proposal and sudden eye widening followed by pupil contraction upon presentation of a name card or photograph.

In interviews, maintain feet parallel, hands at sides or on knees, eyes level, and slight forward lean; adjust posture if the interviewer leans back. Expressions comprise static features and dynamic signals. The seven core expressions are:
- happiness (mouth corners up, crow’s feet)
- contempt (one mouth corner raised)
- disgust (nasal lines)
- anger (furrowed brows)
- sadness (inverted brows, chin wrinkles)
- fear (trembling, tense lower lids)
- surprise (wide eyes and mouth)

### Expressions as universal language

Foot movements and torso orientation reliably indicate emotional states, directing toward liked people or topics and away from disliked ones or exits. Anticipation shows in hand-rubbing or thumb-forefinger rubbing, while anti-gravity behaviors such as floating sensations mark happiness or excitement. Liars often omit personal pronouns and names.

Ekman’s team tested an isolated Papua New Guinea tribe with no Western media exposure; members identified expressions accurately, and Americans recognized the tribe’s photographed expressions. Studies of infants and blind individuals confirmed consistency. As of 2016 the seven universally recognized expressions are happiness, contempt, disgust, anger, sadness, fear, and surprise.

Bayer recorded seven deliberate expressions—anger, fear, seduction, helplessness, happiness, sadness, indifference—with viewers identifying an average of only two. Wolff found left-face changes faster and stronger, reflecting genuine emotion via the right hemisphere, while the right face conveys rational or false signals via the left hemisphere. In the Spring and Autumn period, Chunyu Kun twice read King Hui of Liang’s face and declined to speak, inferring preoccupation first with chariot driving and then with sensual pleasures.

Emotion is a short-lived, coordinated response across language, physiology, behavior, and neural systems to an appraised event, functioning as an evolved survival mechanism. Faced with negative stimuli, animals assess relative strength: weaker stimuli trigger disgust and withdrawal, equal strength triggers anger and attack, and stronger stimuli trigger fear and flight. Positive stimuli elicit joyful excitement. Human emotions arise mainly from social events processed through rules yet retain primitive logic that produces autonomic changes such as elevated heart rate, blood pressure, sweating, and muscle tension in anger.

Upper facial muscles include:

Physiological expressions fall into four categories: comfort (eyes close with upward roll, eyebrows lift proportionally to intensity); pain (orbicularis oculi contracts forcefully, teeth clench, upper lip rises, lower lip pulls down, mouth corners stretch horizontally via platysma); attention (furrowed brows with passive lid descent and directed gaze); and absent-mindedness (upper lids droop to pupil edge with unfocused gaze that follows head rotation).

A psychological stress micro-reaction is an inadvertent, brief, low-amplitude action after an effective stimulus that cannot be fully masked. Examples include persistent shivering and arm tightening despite verbal denial of cold, or a subordinate carrying a bedpan at arm’s length while holding breath after smiling compliance. Only these unmasked traces reliably indicate true state.

### Microexpression foundations

Certain facial muscles resist voluntary control. During the 1982 meeting between Pope John Paul II and Jaruzelski, the Pope’s maintained smile conveyed political acknowledgment. A normal person possesses 44 facial muscles capable of more than 5,000 distinct expressions.

Physiological behaviors directed solely at the self include eating, drinking, defecating, urinating, and sleeping. Only the motor system and diaphragm can be regulated by cortical will; routine diaphragm movement is managed by the autonomic nervous system via the brainstem, medulla oblongata, and pons. Polygraphs therefore measure skin conductance, heartbeat, respiration, and blood volume with high accuracy.

Micro-expressions are evolutionarily inherited, subconscious, and uncontrolled. They last as briefly as 0.04 seconds and are captured by only 10 percent of observers. They appear in national security, judicial systems, clinical medicine, and elections. Seven universal expressions occur across cultures and even among the congenitally blind: happiness, contempt, disgust, anger, sadness, fear, and surprise. Eleven further expressions—shame, humiliation, guilt, apprehension, boasting, joy, pleasure, excitement, reassurance, and satisfaction—also appear common. Micro-expressions last under 0.2 seconds when emotion is suppressed.

Six basic emotions are expressed through facial musculature. Happiness produces a smooth forehead, raised lower eyelids with crow’s feet, bulging cheeks, and lifted mouth corners, with possible teeth exposure. Sadness produces a wrinkled forehead, shortened raised eyebrows forming a triangle between the brows, nasal root and eyes, slack cheeks, and a downturned mouth. Surprise, disgust, anger, and fear follow parallel morphological patterns.

Emotional behaviors follow survival logic: surprise increases sensory intake; disgust prompts withdrawal; anger mobilizes resources; fear triggers hiding or flight; sadness conserves energy; happiness discharges excitation. Variants include revulsion and contempt (disgust); irritation and sternness (anger); apprehension and anxiety (fear); sorrow and guilt (sadness); satisfaction and excitement (happiness).

Fifteen muscle groups govern expressions. Muscles 1–5 center on the orbicularis oculi (upper face); muscles 6–15 center on the orbicularis oris (lower face). The orbicularis oris is the sole unpaired facial muscle, with marginal and peripheral portions meeting at the vermilion border. Its contraction purses or protrudes the lips; opposing forces from levator labii superioris alaeque nasi, zygomaticus major, platysma, depressor anguli oris, depressor labii inferioris, and mentalis produce lip raising, corner elevation or depression, stretching, or parting. Platysma fibers pull mouth corners laterally and downward in crying or pouting. Mentalis originates below the lower lip and inserts into chin skin, bulging the chin and elevating the central lower lip chiefly in sadness. Buccinator expels air between the lips after cheek puffing. Levator labii superioris alaeque nasi dilates the nostril and elevates the upper lip. Levator labii superioris raises and everts the upper lip. Zygomaticus minor raises the upper lip in disgust and sadness. Zygomaticus major pulls the mouth corner upward and laterally. Risorius assists smiling. Depressor anguli oris and depressor labii inferioris pull the mouth corner or lower lip downward and laterally.

Head position signals include: tilted backward for pride; lowered for negation or remorse; suddenly dropped for humility; held straight for neutrality; tilted to one side with nodding for interest; repeated nodding for agreement; suddenly raised for surprise.

False expressions are revealed by facial asymmetry, timing anomalies, and sequencing mismatches. Prolonged expressions are typically false; authentic ones are brief except in extremes
## Part II — The face by region

### Brows

Facial expressions convey thoughts and emotions, intentionally or not, but must be read in clusters with gestures, posture, and speech rather than in isolation. Eyes receive the most attention in interaction, yet every feature contributes. In sign language the same hand signs for “Grandfather is ill” become a query when paired with an urgent expression.

Eyebrows act as a barometer of mood. Raising one signals doubt or incomprehension; raising both indicates strong appreciation, liking, surprise, or an impending question. Gerard Nierenberg’s experiments showed these movements reliably expose psychological states. A single brow raised while the other stays level signals surprise mixed with doubt; resolving the doubt often produces agreement. Lynn Clasen identified more than twenty distinct eyebrow dynamics, each tied to specific emotions. A slight, momentary brow raise during conversation indicates confidence. Professor Mark classified 3,000 adult eyebrow photographs and linked narrow glabellar regions to calculation and wide regions to generosity.

Angry eyebrows form a “10:10 shape”: inner ends drawn together and pressed downward while outer ends rise diagonally. This combines corrugator supercilii activity producing vertical frown lines with frontalis activity lifting the outer brows. Leveling of the outer brows signals subsiding anger. Eyebrow movements arise from frontalis, corrugator supercilii, orbicularis oculi, and depressor supercilii action. They protect the eyes and signal inner states. Anger produces simultaneous frontalis and corrugator contraction, widening eyes, lifting outer brows, flaring nostrils, and quickening breath; the head lowers and chin tucks before attack. Surprise raises brows with matching upper-eyelid elevation. Sadness lowers outer brow ends while lifting inner ends, often with orbicularis contraction that readies tears. An eyebrow flash—rapid bilateral raise and drop—signals welcome or emphasis. One brow raised indicates deliberate doubt. Knitted brows can mark worry, confusion, disgust, or thought, requiring context.

The corrugator supercilii lies at the inner eyebrows and, with the orbicularis oculi, draws the brows together to form vertical creases under strong light, worry, or anger. The depressor supercilii lowers the inner brows and creates horizontal wrinkles at the nasal root. The frontalis raises the brows and produces 2–3 forehead wrinkles in adults, widening the palpebral fissure. The procerus lowers the brows and adds horizontal lines at the nasal root. These muscles generate five primary eyebrow movements: raising, knitting, shrugging, one brow raised with the other lowered, and drawing inward while lifting outward.

### Eyes

Paul Broca identified Broca’s area in 1861, located just behind and above the eyeball; damage produces motor aphasia. Blood flow increases here during speech, and frequent talkers develop slight downward-outward protrusion of the eyeballs. Pupils dilate four- to fivefold during excitement and constrict when calm. Blink rate more than doubles under sudden psychological pressure, as occurs with non-professional liars. Appropriate gaze occupies 30–60 percent of conversation time; exceeding 60 percent signals either attraction or impending conflict.

Three primary sensory systems appear in language and eye behavior:
- Visual: rapid speech, nasal tone, wide pitch variation, preference for visual descriptors; eyes typically move upward and left (right-handers recalling images) or right (constructing images).
- Auditory: slower, measured rhythm, careful word choice, auditory phrases such as “That sounds right.”
- Kinesthetic: slowest delivery, deep voice, kinesthetic phrases such as “I haven’t quite gotten hold of it”; eyes often lower or drift.

Twelve observable eye states supply diagnostic cues: calm and steady indicates a formed plan; scattered and unfocused indicates absence of a solution; sharp sideways glance signals coldness; dark and gloomy signals ruthlessness; atypical movement signals a hidden scheme; dull eyes with pale lips signal acute anxiety; blazing eyes signal anger; tranquil eyes with a smile signal pleasure; darting eyes signal boredom; steady fixation signals receptive attention; lowered eyes with tilted head signal heavy worry; upward gaze signals unwillingness to listen.

Eyelid closure is governed by the orbicularis oculi muscle; elevation of the upper lid by the levator palpebrae superioris (both under voluntary somatic control) and Müller’s muscle (smooth muscle under autonomic control only). Involuntary closure occurs with negative stimuli to reduce impact. Positive stimuli also prompt closure to heighten other senses, producing relaxed expression with minimal brow or mouth change. Negative closure produces discomfort, with furrowed brows, downturned mouth corners, chin folds, and nasal wrinkles involving frontalis, corrugator supercilii, orbicularis oculi, depressor labii inferioris, and orbicularis oris. Rapid blinking arises when facing decisions.

Stanford research divided subjects by eye-turn direction during thirty-second questioning: rightward turns correlated with impatience and aggression; leftward with internalization. In the Bert prison-abuse interview, repeated rightward glances despite courteous answers led agents to provoke him, revealing his violent nature. A test subject’s baseline rightward gaze shifted left only when denying dislike of a friend; follow-up image and micro-expression checks confirmed concealed disgust.

An experiment paired fifty students into twenty-five dyads for twenty seconds of silent pupil-to-pupil contact. At contact, both expected speech; after two seconds most felt awkward; after five seconds many wished to look away; at twenty seconds all reported relief and an urge to speak. People spend 30–60 percent of interaction time in mutual gaze; above 60 percent signals attraction or impending conflict. Extroverts maintain steady contact; sudden prolonged staring by normally shy individuals often indicates lying.

The sphincter pupillae, under parasympathetic control, constricts the pupil; the dilator pupillae, under sympathetic control, dilates it. Both operate outside voluntary control. In one FBI sexual-harassment murder case, the suspect’s pupils dilated when he mentioned the victim, contradicting his denials. Panic or excitement can expand pupils to four times normal size. Six skeletal extraocular muscles—the medial rectus, lateral rectus, superior rectus, inferior rectus, superior oblique, and inferior oblique—move each eye under voluntary control. Abnormal insertion or paralysis produces strabismus. Upward gaze shifts often indicate escape or search; downward shifts indicate endurance or guilt. Normal blink rate is five to eight times per minute. Several blinks within one second signal emotional arousal or shyness; a blink exceeding one second indicates boredom or contempt. ## Part III — Basic emotions on the face

### The seven basic emotions

The breakup scenario triggers surprise at an unexpected statement, followed by disgust at the ex-boyfriend, anger at the threat to the relationship, fear of irreversible loss, and final sadness once the outcome is fixed. Effective stimuli first produce surprise, then a rapid benefit-harm judgment. Positive stimuli yield pleasure of varying intensity. Negative stimuli produce disgust (rejection without threat), anger (urge to attack when threatened), fear (flight or acceptance when threat exceeds capacity), or sadness (after irreversible loss). Paul Ekman confirmed these six universal emotions—surprise, disgust, anger, fear, sadness, pleasure—through fieldwork among isolated New Guinea tribes.

Color influences emotion via natural associations. Green lowers skin temperature ~2 °C, heart rate 4–8 bpm, and calms operating-room staff and patients. Blue treats insomnia and lowers blood pressure. Yellow reduces boredom in factories and promotes cheer. Red energizes but can produce irritability. Purple stabilizes mood in pregnancy. Pink quiets violent patients in institutional settings. Blue and green calm tension and fatigue after work; red, yellow, orange, and pink lift pessimism and depression; pink, light blue, and light yellow stabilize irritability. White cools agitation; black aids insomnia. Prolonged green may induce desolation, and red should be avoided when relaxation is needed.

Emotion precedes reason in response to stimuli, mobilizes bodily energy for survival and reproduction, and is difficult to fake when intense, as in anger’s deepened breathing and accelerated circulation. Subjective expectation is the judgment the nervous system forms about imminent events from all past and present information. It registers as a rapid “feeling” rather than deliberate reasoning. In an office where colleagues avert their eyes, an assistant avoids routine tasks, and the boss draws the blinds, the resulting sense of threat arises without explicit logic. Humans generate such expectations instinctively to serve survival and reproduction. In total darkness, movement slows and limbs extend cautiously because the nervous system assumes possible harmful objects; the unknown itself is not the root of fear—only the expectation of harm is. A blind person forms safer expectations through familiarity. Psychiatric patients who cannot cognize a threat show no fear.

The half-full, half-hungry method maintains optimal pressure. A fully fed chimpanzee ignored food outside its cage and treated the available stick as a toy; a ravenously hungry chimpanzee reached futilely, grew agitated, and abandoned the tool. Only the intermediate state prompted rational use of the stick. The same principle applies to humans: insufficient pressure produces slackness, excessive pressure exceeds limits, and moderate pressure sustains peak performance. Yerkes’ inverted-U links pressure to performance: too little breeds inertia, too much produces anxiety or overload, optimal tension yields motivation.

Music therapy influences emotion through rhythm, melody, tempo, and dynamics. Melodious pieces produce cheer and stability; marches inspire enthusiasm; dirges evoke sadness. It raises cortical excitability, reduces tension, anxiety, and fear, and improves stress resilience. Beethoven’s music shifts sorrow to happiness and timidity to courage. Later sources add Paul Ekman’s research on facial expressions and deception, Joe Navarro and Marvin Karlins’ What Every BODY Is Saying, Allan and Barbara Pease’s The Definitive Book of Body Language, and Gray’s Anatomy (39th edition).

Additional references include A Treatise of Human Nature, The Descent of Man and Selection in Relation to Sex, The Principles of Psychology, Games People Play, Detecting Lies and Deceit, Telling Lies, Emotions Revealed, How to Read a Person Like a Book, Evolutionary Psychology (2nd ed.), Social Psychology (8th ed.), Personality Psychology (4th ed.), and The Psychology of Criminal Conduct. Journals cited include Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, Psychological Monographs, and Experimental Psychology. Anatomical sources draw from Neuropsychology, Physiological Psychology, and Medical Psychology.

Key indexed attitudes and signals cover recognition, offense, anger, resentment, boredom, confidence, cooperation, resistance, disgust, superiority, doubt, worry, expectation, fear, unease, frustration, happiness, surprise, interest, admiration, anxiety, openness, willingness to converse, readiness, self-affirmation, courtship, satisfaction, self-control, contentment, shyness, and concealment.
